# Stormwater Phase II Final Rule - Construction Rainfall Erosivity Waiver

**Document:** Fact Sheet 3.1

**Source:** United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)

**Office:** Office of Water (4203)

**EPA ID:** 833-F-00-014

**Revised:** March 2012

---

## Overview

The Stormwater Phase II Final Rule allows permitting authorities to waive NPDES requirements for small construction sites if the site meets specific erosivity criteria.

### Purpose

The 1972 amendments to the Federal Water Pollution Control Act (Clean Water Act - CWA) prohibit the discharge of any pollutant to navigable waters of the United States unless the discharge is authorized by a National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permit.

**Phase I Requirements:** All construction sites with planned land disturbance of 5 acres or more must obtain an NPDES permit and implement stormwater runoff control plans.

**Phase II Extensions:** The Stormwater Phase II rule extends requirements to sites between 1 and 5 acres.

**Rainfall Erosivity Waiver:** Allows permitting authorities to waive those sites that do not have adverse water quality impacts.

---

## What is Erosivity?

Erosivity is the term used to describe the **potential of soil to wash off disturbed, denuded earth during storms**.

The potential for erosion is determined in part by:
- **Soil type and geology** of the site
  - Fine particles erode less readily than sandy soils
  - Clay-like soils retain water better than sandy soils on the side of a hill
- **Amount and force of precipitation** expected during the construction period
  - In some areas, sufficient precipitation is expected during the construction period
  - There are definite optimal periods, such as a dry season when tends to fall less frequently and with less force
- **When feasible:** Site can be stabilized by the time the seasonal weather returns

### Seasonal and Geographic Variations

There are many other important factors to consider when determining erosivity, such as:
- Freeze/thaw cycles
- Snow pack

## How Is Site Erosivity Determined?

The **Universal Soil Loss Equation (USLE)** was developed by the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) in the 1950s to help farmers conserve their valuable topsoil.

The methodology for determining if a site qualifies for the erosivity waiver is provided in the USDA Handbook 703 - "Predicting Soil Erosion by Water: A Guide to Conservation Planning With the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE)," dated January 1997.

**Note:** A more updated version of USLE, the Revised USLE, Version 2 (RUSLE2) is available and can be used as an alternative method for determining if a site qualifies for the erosivity waiver.

### R Factor (Erosivity Index)

Using a computer model supported by decades of soil and rainfall data, USDA established estimates of annual erosivity values (R factors) for sites throughout the country. These R factors are used as surrogate measures of the impact that rainfall had on erosion from a particular site.

**Erosivity Index Table (EI Table):** Provided here (Table 1) to show how the annual erosivity factor is distributed throughout the year in two-week increments. Table 1 is based on 120 rainfall distribution zones for the continental U.S. Detailed instructions for calculating a project R factor are based on the USDA Handbook 703.

## Waiver Eligibility and Availability

The Stormwater Phase II rule allows permitting authorities to waive NPDES requirements for small construction sites **if the value of the rainfall erosivity factor is less than 5** during the period of construction activity (see § 122.26(b)(15)(i)(A)).

### Important Distinctions

**Note:** This waiver is not available to sites that will disturb **more than 5 acres of land** (large construction).

**Permitting authority options:**
- They have the option to not allow waivers for small construction activity
- They can establish an R factor threshold lower than 5
- They can suspend the waiver within an area where thresholds are known to be heavily impacted by, or sensitive to sedimentation
- They can also suspend the waiver during certain periods of the year

---

## Other Factors Affecting Waiver Availability and Eligibility

EPA has established the R factor of **less than 5** as the criteria for determining waiver eligibility. However, since the intent is to waive only those construction activities that will not adversely impact water quality:

- State and Tribal permitting authorities have considerable discretion in determining when, and how to offer it
- They can establish an R factor threshold lower than 5
- They can suspend the waiver in an area where thresholds are known to be heavily impacted by, or sensitive to sedimentation
- They can also suspend the waiver during certain periods of the year

**Important:** This waiver is not available for sites that will disturb more than 5 acres of land (large construction).

---

## What if My Site Is Not Eligible for a Waiver?

**If your site is not eligible for a waiver**, you must submit a **Notice of Intent (NOI)** or whichever type of application is required, to obtain coverage under the applicable NPDES construction stormwater permit.

For information about EPA's Construction General Permit (CGP), check: [EPA CGP Website](http://www.epa.gov/npdes/stormwater/cgp)

**State program information** is available at: [EPA State Contacts](http://cfpub.epa.gov/npdes/contacts.cfm?program_id=STATE)

## How Do I Compute the R Factor for My Project?

### Step 1: Estimate the construction start date

This is the day you expect to begin disturbing soils, including:
- Grubbing
- Stockpiling
- Excavating
- Grading activities

### Step 2: Estimate the construction end date

This is defined by your permitting authority's regulations or NPDES construction stormwater permit, over all previous disturbed areas. This is your construction end date.

### Step 3: Refer to Figure 1 - Erosivity Index Zone Map

Find your Erosivity Index (EI) Zone based on your geographic location.

### Step 4: Refer to Table 1 - Erosivity Index Table

Find the number of your EI Zone in the left column. Locate the EI values for the dates that correspond to the project start and end dates you identified in Steps 1 and 2.

**If your specific date is not on the table:**
- Use the closest date prior to your proposed start date
- Use the closest date after your proposed end date
- Interpolate between dates in the table to obtain your %EI value

### Step 5: Calculate the percent EI for your site

Subtract the start value from the end value to find the % EI for your site. **The maximum annual EI value for a project is 100%.**

**Example:** If your project lasts for one year or more, your EI value is 100%.

### Step 6: Multiply to get R Factor

Multiply the percent value obtained in Step 5 by the annual isoerodent value obtained in Step 5. This is the **R factor for your scheduled project**.

## Can I Use a Personal Computer to Calculate the R Factor?

The computer program used by USDA to evaluate erosion potential is called the **Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE)**. The current version of RUSLE (RUSLE2) is a Windows-based model that uses extensive databases that are geographically-linked.

**RUSLE2 Usage:**
- Can be downloaded free of charge from the Internet
- Requires a large investment of time to set up
- RUSLE2 can be used to calculate the R factor for a proposed construction site
- However, RUSLE2 also requires the large investment of time to set up

**Alternative Methods:**
- EPA notes that either method of calculation is acceptable for determining eligibility for construction rainfall erosivity waiver
- You can calculate your R factor using:
  - Methods specified in USDA Handbook 703 (Predicting Soil Erosion by Water)
  - More updated RUSLE2 methodology

## Important Notes

- This waiver applies ONLY to construction sites with minimal rainfall erosivity potential during construction
- Not available for sites disturbing 5+ acres
- Permitting authorities have discretion in setting local thresholds
- Must still obtain NOI if not eligible for waiver
- R factor calculation methods are standardized but site-specific
